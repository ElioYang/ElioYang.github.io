Designing Childhood Online

Child online-safety laws are design specifications written by people who are not designers. I code what harm they think they are preventing, and how that idea of harm becomes interface rules.

Undergraduate research assistant · with Dr. Soumita Mukherjee and Prof. Madelyn Sanfilippo, UIUC School of Information Sciences · 2026–present

Work in progress. Findings are preliminary.

Question

Laws written to protect children online rarely talk about privacy in the abstract. They name design features instead: infinite scroll, notifications at midnight, like counts, a stranger's ability to send a direct message. What do these laws think harm is, and how does that idea of harm get translated into concrete interface requirements?

What I did

  • Started from the UK Age Appropriate Design Code, then extended the analysis to five U.S. state laws modeled on it: Maryland, Nebraska, South Carolina, New Jersey, and Vermont.
  • Coded each law against a seven-category privacy-harm typology adapted from Citron and Solove (physical, economic, reputational, psychological, autonomy, discrimination, relationship): about 330 coded excerpts across six instruments, nearly all with an analytic memo naming the risk the provision guards against.
  • Reconstructed Maryland's enacted text from a chaptered bill whose struck-through clauses had leaked into the extracted document, and audited the coding set for excerpts cut off at page breaks.
  • Kept a running log of ambiguous coding decisions (for example, compulsive use and parental monitoring) for the team to resolve together, rather than settling them alone.

What we are seeing (preliminary)

  • Harm as loss of control. Autonomy is the most frequent harm in every jurisdiction. These laws mostly treat a child's privacy as the ability to decide who sees them, what is recommended to them, and when they are interrupted. Part of this reflects our coding conventions (provisions on data minimization and transparency were coded as autonomy), so the next step is to separate harms a law names from harms we infer from its safeguards.
  • From principles to mechanisms. The UK code reasons through "best interests" and developmental stages. The U.S. laws legislate the interface directly: default visibility to adults, notification quiet hours, hidden engagement counts, search-engine de-indexing. Stranger danger has been rewritten as a set of visibility toggles.
  • Compulsive use becomes a legal category. South Carolina, New Jersey, and Vermont define it as use that disrupts major life activities such as sleep, eating, or learning. Designing for engagement now has a legal threshold.
  • Who gets to watch. The laws split on monitoring. New Jersey and Vermont require a visible signal to the minor even when a parent is the one watching; Maryland lets parents monitor without one. The disagreement is about whose autonomy the interface should protect.
  • What the laws avoid. Reputational harm is almost entirely absent. The U.S. laws also repeatedly disclaim any duty over content, which pushes all of the protective work onto design.

Why it matters for design

If we know which harms these provisions encode and which they miss, we can build systems for young people that do more than comply: systems that make algorithmic influence visible, make defaults defensible, and give teenagers control that grows as they do. The open question these laws cannot answer for themselves is whether young people can actually use the controls they mandate. That is a design question, and a testable one.
