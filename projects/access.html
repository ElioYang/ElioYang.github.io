When Access Should Change

When a person's role changes, what they can see and who can see them should change too, and someone should be able to show that it did.

Sole author · Under review, ACM CSLAW 2027

Question

When a 17-year-old takes over her own settings, or a support relationship ends, which grounds for access end, which persist, and what evidence would show that the change was actually carried out?

What I did

  • Modeled each transition in a religious community's member directory as four separate decisions: who manages a setting, who receives information, what is restricted, and what is retained. The case draws on Contextual Integrity and on the public policies of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, in a stipulated UK data-protection setting.
  • Built a single-process Python prototype and evaluated 46 authorization configurations on synthetic data, backed by 80 automated tests.
  • Traced where exposure arises along a transition's timeline: stale cached grants, secondary lookup paths, and plaintext released before encryption was applied.
  • Proposed claim-scoped evidence records: a record that binds a legally classified requirement to a declared query, path, and time scope, and that reports coverage gaps alongside errors.

Result

Staleness was the most tractable problem. A strict lease of b + 1 ticks met a staleness budget of b ticks, and revision checks eliminated observed false grants at an availability cost.

Fixing staleness did not fix everything. A review-first no-contact policy, executed correctly, still permitted all 73 promotional-outreach probes that interim restriction blocked. A secondary path that the probes did not cover served stale grants that primary-path evidence could not see. That is why the evidence record has to say what it did not check.
