Who Sets the Default?

Laws now require "high privacy by default" for children. I study what those defaults should be at each age, and whether young users can see and change them.

Sole-authored papers (SOTICS 2026, PrivaCI 2026), with feedback from Prof. Madelyn Sanfilippo · Default Inspector demo

Question

When a platform applies one privacy baseline to every user, whose expectations does that baseline actually encode, and what happens to the users it was not built for?

What I did

  • Named a recurring pattern, normative flattening: a platform applies the privacy baseline of a digitally skilled adult to users with different skills, risks, and expectations (SOTICS 2026, short paper).
  • Proposed a four-step Contextual Integrity procedure (identify the flow, find the assumed user baseline, compare how different ages interpret it, link the mismatch to design and governance) and applied it to teen visibility and algorithmic amplification.
  • Argued that age changes how people read a flow's transmission principles, rather than being one more attribute of the data subject. Presented at the 8th PrivaCI Symposium (2026).
  • Built on my research-assistant work coding the UK Age Appropriate Design Code and five U.S. state laws modeled on it (see Designing Childhood Online).
  • Worked through a concrete case: the FTC's 2022 action against Epic Games over Fortnite's default-on voice and text chat for children and teens, and what age-tiered defaults should have looked like instead.

Result

The framework turns a vague complaint ("this setting isn't right for kids") into a located mismatch that a designer or regulator can act on. An extended version is invited for the International Journal on Advances in Security (2027).

The Default Inspector demo puts the age-tiered defaults from my Fortnite analysis in front of users and lets them contest each one.
