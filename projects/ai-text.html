Tracing AI-Transformed Text

When an AI rewrites a library text, can we trace it back to its source, and what signal are we really following?

Question

Given an AI-generated derivative, can transparent retrieval methods find the collection record that guided it? And when they succeed, which textual traces are they actually relying on?

What I did

  • Built a controlled benchmark: 60 passages generated from three King James Version chapters by two chat systems, under content-preserving and style-oriented instructions. Each passage queried all 260 New Testament chapters.
  • Compared lexical, character-based, stylometric, and hybrid retrieval.
  • Designed a masking test that removes words in phrases shared with the source (5-, 4-, and 3-grams), to see which methods depend on copied wording.
  • In a companion study (ASIS&T 2026), compared 18 imitations from three generative models with their source chapters across lexical, function-word, and sentence-structure features.

Result

Simple retrieval worked well: Word TF–IDF reached a mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of .967 for content adaptations and .956 for style-oriented rewrites.

The masking test showed what the stylometric method had been following. For style-oriented passages, its MRR fell from .540 to .018 under 3-gram masking, below the random baseline of .024. The apparent "style" signal was carried largely by wording the passages had retained from their source. The ASIS&T study points the same way: imitation is uneven, with lexical markers reproduced relatively closely and sentence-level structure far more variable.

Both studies are small and use one homogeneous collection, so they do not establish a general-purpose detection method.
